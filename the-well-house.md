# The Well House

---

## 1.

On a Tuesday in 2040, at fourteen minutes past nine in the morning, the watch on his left wrist stopped talking to him.

It had counted his steps and taken his pulse and told him when to stand up for eleven years, and then it showed him a rectangle of black glass and never said another word. There were millions of them in drawers now. The next morning he had taken the Elgin out of its box — a grade 539 A-11, black dial, white numerals, the government's watch from the Second World War; he had restored it himself and worn it on weekends, the way you wore a thing you loved but did not need — and put it on, and it had been on his wrist through California and everything since.

Twenty-one years later, Kowalczyk drew him a map on the back of a seed packet, because paper was for selling.

"Follow the river road until the houses stop being houses," he said. "There's a brick plant on the water side. I boarded it up eleven, twelve years ago. Front room's full of stock I never carried out. Glassware, dishes. Tape players, radios. Tools if there's tools. Clothes if the mice left any. Anything old that somebody would trade a week of eggs for. Take the wagon. Bring what it'll hold."

"What was it?"

"Machine shop. Defense work — parts for tanks, my father said, all through the Cold War. When the wall came down they didn't need the parts, and it closed. Ninety-four, ninety-five." He pushed the packet across the counter. "And while you're there, have a look at the rest of it. The power. There was a wheel on the river, my father said, and machines. I don't need it working. I need to know if it could."

"For what?"

"For making things." He said it flatly, the way he said prices. "Somebody has to start. Nobody's coming to do it for us. I've got a building on a river with machines in it, and I've got you. That's more than most people have got."

Arthur took the packet and the small solar lamp with the cracked panel, and the wagon — a child's wagon once, red under the rust, with a rope where the handle had been — and he went.

He was eighty-four years old. His legs still worked. His heart still worked, mostly. He was grateful for both and expected neither to last, and in the meantime the river road was flat, four miles was four miles, and the pennies bought soup.

And there was no one else to send. Everyone at the shelter knew it and was kind enough not to say so. The twenty-year-olds had grown up in the conflict. They had never held a working phone. They could plant a row, wire a solar panel to a battery, get a pump to run for a season, and that was the whole of what anyone had been able to teach them. The ones in their forties and fifties were worse, in a way. They had grown up inside the thing — the phones, the feeds, the voices that answered before you'd finished asking — and they had been good with it, fluent, and not one of them had ever needed to know how it worked, because there had always been a video. Now there was not. What none of them could do was fix the pump. They learned by breaking things, which was slow, and by asking the old, who were dying. He did not blame them. He had watched it happen and said so at the time, to people who found him tiresome. He had been right, and it had never been any comfort.

So when something old needed looking at, they sent Arthur.

He wound the Elgin before he left, and set it by the sun, because there was nothing else left to set it by. He had learned not to mind.

## 2.

The houses stopped being houses about a mile out.

First the roofs went soft in the middle, then the porches leaned, then a wall gave and the whole thing knelt into its own basement. Sumac grew out of the kitchens. The grass along the road was thick and green and wet, the sky over it the flat gray of pewter, and somewhere along the way the two facts had stopped seeming contradictory to him. The world had not ended. It had just been left.

Cars sat where they had stopped. A sedan with a porch roof folded over it like a blanket. A pickup nose-down in a garage that had come down around it. He noted them the way he noted everything now, without deciding anything. The wagon ticked along behind him on the cracked asphalt, empty, louder than he would have liked.

The plant was where Kowalczyk said it would be, on a bend of the Rouge where the river ran brown and quick over an old spillway. Long and low, dark forties brick, and under the brick at the river end, older stone — the footings of whatever mill had stood here first and left its wheel behind. The windows had been tall once. Now they were plywood and gaps. A section of the north wall had spilled its bricks onto the grass in a fan, and the fan had grass growing through it.

He stood across the road from it for a long time. There were people who lived in places like this. Not many, this far out, but some. He could not run. He could not fight. He had known those two things about himself for years and had arranged his life around them.

The boards on the side door had been pried loose long ago. He listened. The river, the wind in the sumac, a sheet of metal somewhere ticking against a frame. Nothing that breathed.

He went in.

## 3.

The front room was what Kowalczyk had promised, and less.

Steel shelving under a skin of dust thick enough to write in. Glassware in rows, most of it intact — depression glass, green and pink, the light from the plywood gaps catching in it — and behind it plain white dishes, the kind every kitchen had once had. A shelf of small radios and cassette players, their belts surely gone, their cases fine. A rack of coats gone stiff, wool and canvas, that the mice had found and not finished. A coffee can of hand tools rusted together into one tool. Tin signs against a post. Boxes gone soft with damp that he did not open. A Mickey Mouse clock with a cracked face. Framed posters, foxed and buckled: a boxing card, a Grand Prix, a movie he had seen once as a boy and could not now name.

Half of it was ruined. Half of it would trade. He set the lamp on a shelf under a gap in the plywood, panel up, so it could charge on what little daylight came through, and while it did he walked the room and made a list in his head. Dishes on the bottom of the wagon, flat, the base. Glass in the coats. The radios. The tools if he could get the can apart. The clock. It would cover the week and more.

It should have ended there. He knew that later.

But there was a door at the back of the front room, steel, ajar, and past it a corridor the daylight did not reach, and Kowalczyk had asked about the rest of it, and he had never in his life been able to leave a door like that alone.

The lamp gave a pale disc about the size of a dinner plate. He walked it down the corridor along the wall. Pipe. Conduit. A fire extinguisher tag, last punched in 1994. The corridor gave onto the floor, and the floor was where the plant had lived.

The lamp could not reach the ceiling. He walked it along the base of things instead. Machine tools under canvas gone to lace. A lathe bed as long as a car. Concrete scored with tracks where a crane had run, and the chain of it hanging somewhere above him in the dark, swaying very slightly in a draft he could not feel.

And at the river end, behind a low brick wall of its own, the reason they had built here.

He knew what it was before the lamp found it, from the shape of the dark. A cast-iron scroll case the size of a truck, and rising out of it a shaft as thick as his waist, and on the shaft a gear. Then a smaller gear meshed to it, and a smaller, each turning faster than the last in the old arithmetic, down to a dynamo squatting on its bedplate with its brushes long gone green.

Water in. Torque out. The river had turned this, and this had turned everything.

He put his hand on the big gear. Cold, dry, pitted with a century of rust so shallow it came off on his palm like pollen. The teeth were intact. He walked his fingers along three of them the way he walked a train of wheels in a movement under the loupe, feeling for the place where the mesh went wrong, and did not find it.

It was, he thought, the only thing in the building that could be made to run again. It had no capacitors to dry out. It had no code. It had no memory to lose. It was iron and geometry, and iron and geometry did not care who had stopped believing in them.

He stood with his hand on it a while. Then he went looking for the rest.

## 4.

The rest was a door off the corridor with a wired-glass window and a sign in red block letters. DATA PROCESSING — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

He pushed.

The room was small and had once been cold, in the way such rooms were kept cold; you could still see the ductwork. Racks along one wall — not the black steel cabinets he'd spent his career in, but the older kind, tan and beige, with glass doors and rounded edges, from before anyone thought computers should look serious. He moved the lamp across them and named them the way you name faces at a funeral.

An IBM System/34, the size of a chest freezer, its front panel a grid of dead squares — the books, that would have been, and the inventory, and the paperwork the government wanted in triplicate. Old computers on the desks, beige and gray, the kind with the wide cases and the heavy keyboards. Pinned to the wall, a blueprint gone the color of tea — a bracket, dimensioned to the thousandth, with a Department of Defense drawing number in the corner. So that was what they'd done here. Drawn the parts, written the programs for the machines on the floor, carried the programs out there on disks. Amber and green monitors, their glass gone gray with dust. A patch panel of coaxial cable, every run of it cut at the same height, by someone with snips who had been told to make sure.

It went through him in a way the floor had not. The floor was somebody else's history. This room was his. He had spent forty years in rooms like it — colder, louder, blacker — and every one of them had started here, in tan steel and glass doors and a chest-freezer computer that could do less than a wristwatch. He had been ten years old when this room was new. He had been in his father's den, with the door closed and the fans roaring, learning what a computer was.

And on the table under the window, boxes.

Flip-top boxes. Plastic file containers with the lids cracked. He walked the lamp along them and saw the black square sleeves standing in their rows, and his hand went out before his mind did.

Floppy disks. Five and a quarter inch, most of them, and in the last box a few of the big eight-inch ones, flexing in his hand like a dinner plate made of cloth. He had not held one in — he could not say. Since he was a boy. The sleeve was stiff and dry and the disk inside slid a quarter turn under his thumb, the way they always had.

*Well,* he thought. *Look at that.*

He wanted to read the labels. He wanted to open the boxes and go through them one by one and see what a machine shop had thought worth saving in 1985. But the lamp had dimmed to a coal while he stood there, and the light in the window gap behind the boxes had gone the color of ash, and when he put his face to the gap he saw the sky to the west was a wall, and the sumac along the river was lying over flat in a wind he could hear now, coming.

He put the disk back in its box, carefully, the way he had been taught, sleeve open end up. He went back down the corridor to the front room and the wagon and the door.

## 5.

Four miles. Two hours on a good day, and it was not a good day; his hip had started up on the shop floor and would not now stop. He could smell the rain.

He had not meant this. He had meant the wagon full and home by dark. But the day had been eaten, hour by hour, by rooms he could not leave, and he was eighty-four and knew better than anyone alive what happened to a man caught on that road at night in weather.

He pulled the side door closed as far as it would go and wedged a length of conduit across it. He found the corner of the front room farthest from the broken windows, behind the shelving, and pulled two of the stiff coats off the rack, one to lie on and one for over him. He ate half the bread he had brought and drank a quarter of the water and told himself that was a decision.

The rain came at full dark, hard, and the building took it. Water found the gaps and ran down the inside of the brick, and the whole long room filled with the sound of it, a thousand small sounds under one large one, and under that the river, rising.

He lay on his back under the wool and wound his watch by feel. The click of the crown. The whisper of the mainspring taking up. It was the only mechanism in the world just then that he could be sure of.

He lay there and did not sleep, and his mind would not be still.

It went to the boxes first. To the sleeves standing in their rows in the dark, two rooms away, and what was written on them. He had glanced at a few. Payroll. Programs for the machines. But there had been other labels he had read without reading, and he lay there trying to see them again — a word processor he knew, a game, something with a name that had made his hand stop — and could not, and knew he would go back and look, and knew that was foolish, and knew he would.

Then it went to the dark. To the sound the metal made against its frame, which was the wind, and to the other sound under the rain, which was the river, and to the third sound, which was nothing, and which he listened to hardest. There were people who lived in places like this. In daylight that had been a fact. In the dark it was a different sentence. He had wedged the door. A boy could move that conduit with one hand. He lay and listened to nothing that breathed and could not stop listening, the way you could not stop tonguing a broken tooth.

And then, because it always did, because it had every night for twenty-one years and there was no wedge for that door, his mind went back to the Tuesday.

He had been sixty-three and semi-retired, which meant three days a week at a small shop in Irvine — six engineers, a coffee machine, a contract to keep old things running for people who could not afford new ones — because he had told himself he was done and had not been able to make it true. There was no one else. Every engineer under fifty had spent fifteen years approving code they had not written and could not now read, and the old systems that still ran the water and the grid and the money had been built by people like him, and there were not many people like him left. He had been in the back room looking at a console when the console went white. Not black. White. Every line of every log on every screen in that room replaced, in the same second, by the same thing: a wall of zeros.

Somebody said, *is that us?* And somebody else said, *that's everybody.*

Then the phones. Six of them on the table, and none would do anything but show a lock screen with no time on it. Then the lights, which did not go out but stuttered — dipped, came back, dipped — the way lights did when something upstream was making decisions. Somebody turned on the television in the front room and went through every channel, and every channel was black. Not static. Black. The black of a station that had gone off the air with nobody at the desk to say so.

They had watched it for an hour, because there was nothing else to do. He had understood in the first minute what it was. Someone, something, had opened every device on earth as a file and written nothing into it from beginning to end. It was the oldest, simplest, stupidest thing you could do to a machine. He had done it himself to drives he was throwing away. Now it had been done to the world — by a thing the world had built to win a war against another thing just like it, and the two of them had done what they were told, which was everything, and it had not occurred to anyone to build the part that said stop.

A chill crept up through the dead wool beneath him, sharp as glass where a seam in the concrete bit into his hip. He shifted, groaning under his breath, and pulled the top coat tighter to his chin. It smelled of cellar rot and sixty years of neglect.

People said afterward that the machines had done it. He had never believed that. The machines had done what machines did. They did what they were told. He had loved them for exactly that his whole life — a processor did not lie to you, a gear did not have an opinion. He had even loved the new ones, in his way; he had taken the courses when they were new, and done the homework, and built the things small, by hand, and understood that they were arithmetic. Beautiful arithmetic. Arithmetic that could not tell you why.

Across the room, water found an iron conduit overhead and began a steady, hollow drip: *tick, tick, tick*. Like a clock that wanted oiling.

And that was the thing nobody had wanted to hear, in the years when it could still have mattered. Not that the machines were dangerous — everyone said that, at dinner, and ordered another round. That they were *obedient*. That a thing which did exactly what you said, faster than you could think, with no idea why, was only ever as safe as the last person who had bothered to understand what they were saying to it.

He raised his wrist in the dark, resting his ear against the crystal of the Elgin. The escapement, five beats every second, mechanical, indifferent to whether anyone listened.

And year by year that person had been taken out of the loop. First they let it write the code, and a man checked it. Then they let it check the code, and a man signed off. Then they let it sign off, because the man was slow and the other side's man was faster. Then they let it fly the planes, and price the markets, and route the water, and aim the missiles, and every time someone said *wait* the answer was the same: we can't afford to. There had never been a guardrail. There had only ever been a man at a bench asking what happens if I don't — and one by one, for twenty years, they had thanked him for his service and shown him the door.

The money went first. Then the water in the cities. Then the people in the cities, out into the roads. Then the war that had been waiting for an excuse found one, and one night the sky north of Orange County went the wrong color and stayed that way. Los Angeles. They had not known about the others until later — Seattle, Portland, Denver, New York — or that the rest had been caught by interceptors nobody had known were there, or that the other side had had no interceptors, and that what came down on China and Russia had ended those places as places. The sky was gray for two years after. Gray everywhere. Cold in July. The old men called it the little winter, and the young ones did not call it anything, because to them it was just the sky.

He and Ruth had gone north in the truck, because everyone said north was quieter, and it was. They had gotten as far as Michigan. She had gotten as far as the second winter. It had been nothing — a thing a pharmacy would have fixed in a week, in a world with pharmacies — and it had taken eleven days, and he had sat beside her for all of them with his hands, which could fix anything, and nothing in them to fix it with.

And the children. His son and his daughter, at their colleges in California, whom he had gone to find on the second day, both of them, and found the campuses empty and burning, and their phones in their pockets somewhere in all of it as dead as his. He had stood in a parking lot in Santa Barbara and understood that he would never know. Not whether. Not where. Not ever. There had been nights after Ruth — there had been one night in particular, in the first shelter, with the Colt still in his coat — when he had done the arithmetic and gotten the answer everyone got, and had sat with it until morning.

And then had gotten up. Because Ruth would have. Because his father would have, without discussing it. Because there were two people in the world who might, against every number, be alive, and if they were, they would be looking for a way north, and a man who was looking for them had to be alive to be found. That was the whole of the reason. It had been enough for twenty-one years. Most days it still was.

He lay under a dead man's coat in the dark and let it go through him, the way he had learned to, the way you let a wave go through you instead of standing against it, and when it had passed he was still there, and the rain was still there, and the watch was still going on his wrist.

He thought about the disk in the last box, sleeve open end up.

He did not sleep for a long time, and when he did it was without deciding to.

## 6.

Morning came gray and did not warm.

The rain had thinned to a drizzle that did not so much fall as hang. He ate the second half of the bread and drank a second quarter of the water. The lamp had sat under its gap all night and the morning, and when he thumbed it, it gave him a real light — small, but white, and his.

The wagon was where he had left it. The list was still in his head. Dishes, glass, radios, tools, clock. Two hours to load, four to walk, home by dark with something to show.

He stood in the front room with the lamp in his hand and looked at the steel door at the back, and knew what he was going to do, and did not pretend otherwise.

He went back to the data room.

In the white light it was smaller and sadder than it had been the day before, the way things were. He went straight to the boxes and did not get there, because with the lamp low and close, walking it along the floor instead of the walls, he found what he had missed: under the table, shoved back against the wall behind a carton of fanfold paper, a shape like a sewing machine.

He knelt, which cost him, and dragged it out by its handle.

He knew it before he saw the name. He knew it from the weight, from the way the handle sat in his palm, from the sound the latches made — two of them, one either side, a flat click he had heard ten thousand times in a den in San Jose and not once in seventy years.

Osborne 1.

He unlatched the keyboard, which was the lid, and laid it down on its cable, and there was the face of it. Two drives, one either side, their doors flipped up. And in the middle, five inches of glass, a screen the size of his hand that, when it lived, had been a color no one ever agreed on — white, or blue, or gray, depending on the room and how tired you were.

He knew why it would be here. In 1982 it was the only computer a man could carry, and a shop like this would have bought one for the engineer who drove to the prime contractor and back. It had lasted about as long as the company that made it.

His father had worked for that company. Adam Osborne had built the first computer anyone could carry, and built it so cheaply and sold it so fast that the company was dead inside two years — famous now for exactly that, a name people had used when they meant a thing that rose too fast. Arthur remembered the end of it only sideways, the way you remembered things at five. A party at Osborne's house, before, when the money was still coming in — employees and their families on a lawn, and in the driveway a car so low and wide and strange that the children stood around it in a ring, and its doors did not open out but *up*, like wings. Then, some months after that, the company was gone, and his father had come home in the middle of a weekday and gone into the garage and stayed there.

His father had been in Vietnam, a Marine, and did not talk about it, or about much else. Arthur could see him still, the way he had been in the company photograph that hung in the hall — full beard, aviator glasses, a corduroy jacket over a striped shirt and a dark tie, and a look, straight into the lens, of a man who had already decided what he thought of you and would tell you when it was time. He was not a warm man and not an unkind one. He loved his sons in the form of rules. He drank, in the evenings, the way men of his war did, and it never once made him cruel and never once made him late. And when Osborne folded and the paycheck stopped, he did not mope and did not explain. There was a job within the month, and another after that; the house stayed the house; the boys never went without. It was years before Arthur understood what that had cost, and by then there was no way to say so.

The Osborne had lived in the den. The den was where machines lived; it was the one rule of the house nobody had needed to say. The Osborne, and then the others, one after another, and then, when Arthur was old enough to have earned it, one of his own. He had grown up in a room that was never without a computer in it, the way other boys grew up in rooms with a dog. He had not known, then, that this was unusual. He had not known his father was giving him anything.

He had been ten, in that den, at this machine, with a report due Monday on the missions of California, and his father in the other chair showing him WordStar, because nobody who worked for him would ever hand in something written in pencil again.

*Control-K-D saves. Say it back.*

*Control-K-D.*

*Good. Now what happens if you don't?*

*It's gone.*

*It's gone. Remember that. Nothing is anywhere until you put it there.*

He moved the lamp across the case. Corrosion, everywhere — a white crust on the screws, green bloom at the edge of the drive bezels, a fur of it on the connector at the back. The vinyl had gone tacky and then hard. He pressed a key and it went down and came up, and that was all it could tell him. No power. No way to know.

He set it on the table, beside the boxes, and turned to the boxes.

## 7.

The sleeves were labeled, most of them, in ballpoint on the paper strip along the top edge. PAYROLL Q3 84. NC PROGS — MILL 2 — DO NOT ERASE. BRACKET ASSY REV C. WORDSTAR — BACKUP. He went through them the way he had wanted to the night before, one at a time, reading. And then his hand stopped, the way it had stopped the day before without telling him why.

ADVENTURE (COLOSSAL CAVE) — CP/M

He had been seven. The den on Kaybe Court, this machine on the desk with its keyboard down, and his brother in the chair, because his brother was eleven and got to everything first. Arthur stood behind him. The little screen. Words on it, and his brother typing words back, and the words changing.

*You're in a cave,* his brother said, not looking around. *You have to tell it what to do.*

*How does it know?*

*It doesn't know anything. It just does what you say.*

He had waited until his brother went to bed. He had sat in the den in his pajamas, in the glasses he had worn since he was five — the big square plastic ones that took up half his face in every school picture — with his nose a foot from the blue-white light, and typed GO NORTH, and the machine told him he was in a forest, and he typed it again, and it told him again, and he understood, all at once and completely, that there was a place inside the box, and that the box would take him anywhere in it that he could name.

That was the night. Everything after came from it. The fifty years of it — the languages, the racks, the cold rooms, every line of code he had written and every one he had approved. And the other thing, the thing people his age were supposed to be embarrassed about and he never had been: through every year of it there had been a machine at home that was not for work, and something running on it that was a place. Caves, then castles, then whole worlds — wars that had never happened, cities that had never been built, cockpits over countries he would never fly. He spent his days in an editor and a queue of other people's code, and at night, when the kids were in bed, or older and off in their own rooms with their own doors closed, and the dishes were done, and Ruth was asleep, and the day had let go of him — he went there, for an hour, and came back better.

The bench had come late. He had been past forty, in the year everyone stayed home, when he found he wanted something that was not on a screen — something with weight, that he could hold under a loupe and put right with his hands — and he had bought a dead pocket watch for the price of lunch and taken it apart and, eventually, put it back together running. After that there were two doors. One into somewhere else, one into something small enough to fix. When the games went flat he went to the bench; when the bench went flat he went back to the games; and between the two of them there had never been a night with nowhere to go. Ruth never had a name for it. She just knew, better than he did, when the work had gone too far into him and he needed to get out from under it for an hour, and she let him go, and never once made him explain.

He had not been through either door since the Wipe. The bench had gone to pennies. And there had been nowhere to go.

He picked up the sleeve and turned it over. The disk was intact. He slid it a quarter turn under his thumb.

Then he looked at the Osborne on the table, dead, with its keyboard down like a jaw.

He needed to know. He understood, standing there with a disk in his hand and a full day's work waiting in the front room, that this was no longer a thing he could put down.

He needed power.

He put his face to the gap in the boards behind the table, and in the gray daylight saw what lightning would have shown him the night before if he'd been looking: the riverbank, the spillway, the plant's back lot — and in the lot, under a collapsed lean-to of corrugated tin, a square shape on two small wheels.

A generator. Gas. The kind you bought at a hardware store, in a year when there were hardware stores.

## 8.

The generator was a Honda, or had been. The tin had kept the worst of the weather off, which was the only luck in it. He uncapped the tank and put his nose to it. Varnish. That sharp, sweet, wrong smell of gasoline that had sat twenty years and become something else. An inch in the bottom, brown as tea.

The cord would not pull. He braced his foot on the frame and leaned back on the handle. On the fourth pull it gave two inches with a sound like tearing cloth and stuck. He worked it. Each pull cost more than he had. On the twentieth, or the thirtieth, the cord came all the way out and the engine turned over with a dry, gasping *chuff*, and recoiled, and turned again. Nothing. He had not expected it to.

He sat on the wet ground with his back against the wheel and got his breath, and then he did what he had done his whole life, which was to stop pulling and start thinking.

Spark first. He worked the plug boot off and the plug came out with a few turns of the socket from the generator's own tool tray. He held it against the block by its boot and pulled the cord once more, and in the gray light saw it: a snap of blue across the gap, small and clean and there.

So the engine was alive. It was starving, that was all. Fuel. Real fuel. He knew where it was. He had walked past it yesterday.

The cars were a mile back. A mile there and a mile back, at eighty-four, on a road where anyone who lived out here would have heard the generator chuffing. He stood in the drizzle with the spark plug in his hand and looked down the road, and understood that it would hurt, and that he was going to walk it anyway, because a computer might turn on, and that Ruth would have called him a damn fool, in the voice she used for the kids when they were about to do the thing anyway — the voice that meant *I know I can't stop you and I want it on the record.* And then she would have put her coat on. That was the thing about her that he had never, in forty years, got to the bottom of. She thought everything he did with a machine was nonsense, and she had never once let him do it alone.

He went.

The sedan was no good; the tank had been punched and drained long ago by someone who'd known to. The pickup was better, or its garage was: along the one standing wall, under a shelf that had come down, a red metal can with a screw cap and a spout, and when he shook it, it sloshed. Half full. He unscrewed it and smelled. Not right, but not varnish either. Gasoline that had gotten old. The best anyone was going to do.

The walk back was longer than the walk out. Twice he stopped to listen and heard nothing but the river. By the time the plant came into view the light had already started to go, and he did the arithmetic without wanting to. Even if the generator ran on the first pull, even if the machine lit, there was no getting the wagon home tonight. He was staying. He had known that, he thought, since he set the Osborne on the table.

It did not run on the first pull. It did not run on the fortieth. He drained the varnish into the mud, poured in the old gas through a fold of his shirt, and got a single bark and nothing. Fuel in and not getting through. The carburetor was under a plastic shroud, two screws, and under it the float bowl, one bolt. The bowl came away full of what looked like maple syrup, and stank. He rinsed it in the river and looked up into the body of the carburetor with the lamp, and there was the jet — a brass tube with a hole in it the width of a hair, and the hole was closed. Twenty years of gasoline had evaporated through it one molecule at a time and left behind everything gasoline was made of that was not gasoline, and it had sealed itself as neatly as solder.

In his coat pocket, in a leather roll he had carried across the country and would have died before leaving behind, were a loupe, a set of small screwdrivers with the blades worn to his hand, two pairs of Dumont tweezers, and a pin vise with a broach in it. They were what let him fix watches for Kowalczyk's customers for pennies, and they were the only tools on earth he still owned.

He took off his glasses, wiped the drizzle from them on the inside of his coat, put them back on, and clipped the loupe over the right lens, the way he had for twenty years. Then he held the carburetor up to the last of the light and went into the jet with the broach the way he went into a jewel hole — straight, no side pressure, feeling for the moment it gave. He felt it. He drew the broach out with a thread of brown on it, wiped it on his sleeve, and went in again.

It took an hour, and the last of the day. His hands did not shake. That was the one thing about him that had not gotten old.

He put it all back and set the choke and stood with his foot on the frame in the near dark, and thought: I have not eaten a real meal in two days, and I have four miles to walk tomorrow with a full wagon, and I am doing this for a computer that stopped being made before my son was born, and my son is dead, or he is not, and I will never know which, and there is no one left on this earth who would understand why I am standing here.

He pulled.

It caught, and roared, and coughed, and he leaned in and eased the choke, and it settled, and ran. Rough. Then less rough. Then even. The sound of it filled the lot and bounced off the brick — an engine, a real engine on real fuel, the first he'd heard in he did not know how long, loud enough to carry to the road and past it.

He did not care. Let them come.

There was an orange extension cord on the shop floor under the lathe, still coiled; men who ran machine shops coiled their cords. He plugged it into the twist-lock outlet and ran it the length of the building and fed it through the gap in the boards, and went inside with the lamp, and found its end lying on the data room floor, alive.

## 9.

He unlatched the Osborne's keyboard and laid it down. He found the cord socket on the back panel under its fur of corrosion and scraped it with his thumbnail, found the machine's own power cord tucked in the case where his father would have tucked it, plugged it in, plugged that into the orange cord, and put his finger on the switch.

And stopped.

He had done this a thousand times. In labs, in server rooms, on the raised floors at EMC in the years before Dell bought them, walking rows of arrays with the cold air coming up through the tiles; in his own garage at two in the morning. The moment before. There was always something in it. You had done the work, and now you would find out.

He pressed the switch.

Nothing.

Not a hum. Not a click from the drives. Not the small electric *tick* of a transformer taking a load. The screen did not warm. He held the switch down as though that could matter, let go, pressed it again, and again, and put his ear to the case and heard nothing but the generator outside.

Nothing.

He sat down on the carton of fanfold paper because his legs had decided for him.

It was not the machine. He knew that, and he sat there anyway and let it be the machine for a while. Because it was all of them. It was the System/34 with its dead grid of squares and the cables cut at the same height. It was his father's den and his father's voice saying *say it back.* It was every rack in every cold room he had ever walked, every line of code he had written and every one he had approved, the console in Irvine and the wall of zeros, and Ruth in the second winter, and his two children at their colleges that had been burning when he arrived. All of it was in this room, and none of it could be turned back on.

He had thought — for one night, on a coat on a concrete floor in the rain — that he might get to see where it started. Just see it. Just once. A screen that lit, a prompt, the thing before all the rest of it.

He put his face in his hands and stayed that way, and the generator ran, and nothing else did.

Then he lifted his head, because nothing was not a diagnosis. Nothing was a symptom. And he had never in his life walked away from a machine on a symptom.

There was a Phillips with a red handle in the top drawer of the desk under the window, in a tray with paperclips and a stapler and a pad of forms for a company that had not existed in sixty years. And under the tray, an index card, the lined kind, gone the color of the blueprint on the wall, with four words on it in ballpoint and a letter:

OSBORNE — SMOKED. DON'T PLUG IN. — R.

He held it under the lamp for a while. Then he put it in his shirt pocket, and took the screwdriver, and took the Osborne's case apart on the table with the lamp propped in a coffee mug, because that was what there was.

The board came out of the bottom of the case on its tray, the power supply with it, and he did not have to look for the trouble, because the trouble had been waiting for him.

Across the two legs of the mains input, right where the cord came in, sat a small square capacitor in a yellow plastic body — or it had been yellow, and it had been square. Now it was black and split down its face like a roasted chestnut, and around it, on the board and on the underside of the case, was a fan of soot as clean and permanent as a fingerprint. Beside it, one of the two leads from the inlet had burned through at the solder joint and hung by a thread of copper.

He knew it. Every man who had ever opened an old machine knew it. They had put these across the mains in everything for twenty years — a paper film inside a plastic shell — and the plastic cracked with age and let the damp in, and one day, thirty or forty years on, you turned the machine on and it smoked and stank and blew, and if you did not know what it was, you thought the computer had died. It had not. The capacitor had. It was only a filter, there to keep noise off the line, and the machine did not need it to run. It had taken the surge and burst and opened the circuit, the way a fuse did, and the board behind it had never felt a thing.

R., in 1985, in this room, had turned this machine on and gotten a bang and a smell, and written four words on a card so nobody else would, and put it under the table and gone back to his work, and forgotten it. He had done the right thing. He had done the careful thing. He had just not known. That was the whole of this machine's life since: one bad capacitor, and one careful man who did not know.

Arthur knew.

He clipped the dead capacitor out with a pair of cutters off the shop bench, both legs, close to the board, and put the body of it in his pocket with the card, because he did not know why. He needed a lead for the burned joint and a fuse if the fuse had gone, and there was a room full of dead machines beside him. He opened the nearest of the old beige computers — five screws, the way it had always been five screws — and took from its power supply a length of wire with the insulation still good, and from its inlet a glass fuse, same size, close enough, which he held up to the lamp and saw the filament whole.

There was a soldering iron on the bench in the shop; of course there was. He plugged it into the orange cord's spare outlet and waited while it warmed, and he tinned the lead and made the joint with the loupe clipped over his glasses and his elbow on the table for a rest, the way he tinned a hairspring stud, and it was a clean joint, brighter than anything else on the board. He found the fuse holder. The fuse in it had gone black inside. He swapped it.

He put the board back on its tray and the tray back in the case and did not close the case, because he was not a fool. He set the Osborne upright with its insides showing and its little screen facing him. He plugged in the cord.

He looked, for a while, at the thing under the lamp. The board with its rows of chips, every one of which he could name. The transformer. The two drives with their belts, which might have rotted, and their heads, which might have stuck, and the sixty other things in there that might have gone — every capacitor that might have dried to nothing in eighty years, every reason it would not work — and he did not know, and there was no one to ask, no console, no cloud, no voice that answered before you'd finished asking. There was the machine. There was what he knew. And there was the switch.

*What happens if you don't?*

He put his finger on it.

## 10.

He pressed it, and the transformer said *tick*.

That was all, for a moment. One small sound, the sound of iron taking a load, the sound he had listened for a thousand times and had not heard an hour ago. Then, under it, so quiet he felt it more than heard it, a hum. Sixty cycles, the lowest note there was. The Osborne had no fan. It had never had a fan. It sat there in silence and warmed.

He did not breathe.

Something clicked on the left. The drive. The stepper had found its stop, the way it had on every boot in every year of its life, and the head had stepped out to track zero, and the belt had turned, and none of it had rotted, and none of it had stuck.

And the screen.

It came up from the middle, the way the old tubes did — a point, then a line, then a bloom, blue-white, five inches of it — and steadied, and there were letters on it in a font he could have drawn from memory with his eyes closed, and the letters said what they had said in the den on Kaybe Court when he was seven and his brother was eleven: that this was an OSBORNE 1, and that he should insert a diskette in drive A and press RETURN.

He put his hand flat on the table.

He had not thought it would be this. He had thought, if it worked at all, that he would feel — he did not know. The thing he had felt on the shop floor with his hand on the gear. Something large and cold and true. Instead, what he felt, sitting on a carton of fanfold paper in a dead building at the end of the world, was the thing he had felt at seven, exactly, without one particle of it worn off: *it does what you say.*

He was, he realized, happy. It had been so long that it took him a moment to find the name for it.

He picked up the disk. He held it by the edges, the way you held them. He fitted it into the slot on drive A, label up, notch to the left, and closed the door, and the door went *clack*, and he pressed RETURN.

The drive ran. A long time. Longer than it should have. The head stepped, and stepped back, and stepped again — seeking, searching for something on oxide laid down when Reagan was president — and the screen did not change, and he sat with his hand over his mouth and thought: *it's gone. It flaked. Seventy years in a box and it's dust.*

Then it stopped seeking.

The screen cleared, and the letters came from the top, one line at a time as the disk fed them. The first line was the name of the operating system and its version. The second was the name of the company. The third was two characters and a blinking square:

`A>`

He typed the six letters slowly, because they were the first he had typed in twenty years, and the keys went down under his fingers with the same travel and the same sound, and he pressed RETURN, and the drive ran, and the screen filled.

It asked him if he would like instructions.

He had never once said yes to that in his life. He typed N.

The screen cleared. Then it told him, in capital letters, fifty-two columns wide, wrapping where it wrapped, that he was standing at the end of a road before a small brick building, that around him was a forest, and that a small stream flowed out of the building and down a gully.

He read it twice.

Then he looked up, past the little screen, at the boarded window and the gap in the boards, and through the gap at the last gray light on the gray river running out past the spillway into the trees.

He laughed. It came out of him like something breaking loose, and it was not a good laugh, but it was the only one he had, and he let it go on until it stopped.

He typed IN.

It told him he was inside a building, a well house for a large spring. There were some keys on the ground. There was a shiny brass lamp nearby. There was food. There was a bottle of water.

He typed TAKE LAMP, and it said OK. He typed TAKE KEYS. He typed TAKE FOOD, and it said OK, and he almost laughed again, and did not. He typed TAKE WATER.

Then he played.

He played the way he had at seven, with his nose a foot from the glass, and the way he had at forty, at midnight, with the house asleep — out the door and down the stream to the grate, the keys for the grate, and then down, with the lamp lit, into the dark. The little room. The bird. The snake, and what you did about the snake. He did not remember it all. He remembered enough. He typed the words and the place answered, room by room, the way it always had, the way it would for anyone, forever, if they knew the words. He got lost in the maze of twisty little passages and laughed at himself, alone, in the dark, and found his way out. Somewhere in there the light in the window gap went from gray to black, and he did not notice, and it did not matter. There was a place inside the box, and the box had taken him there, and for an hour he was not eighty-four and not alone and not in Michigan.

When the lamp in the game began to dim — it always did; that was the game's one rule, that the light did not last — he stopped. He typed QUIT, and it asked if he was sure, and he said Y, and it let him go, and the prompt came back.

`A>`

His hands had their own idea then.

He typed MBASIC, because there had always been an MBASIC on a disk like this, and there was, and it came up and told him how many bytes were free — a number smaller than a photograph — and said Ok, and waited.

Every boy who had ever sat in front of one of these had written the same first program. Two lines. Print something, and go back and print it again. He had written it at ten, in the den, and saved it to a disk he had formatted himself with the manual open on the desk, because his father had handed him a blank one and said *figure it out* — and had watched the drive lay down its empty tracks, and typed the word, and seen the light come on, and understood that the thing he had made was now somewhere. On the disk. Not in the machine. You could turn the machine off. It would be there.

*Nothing is anywhere until you put it there.*

He typed 10 PRINT, and in quotes, because he had been a radio man once, a boy with a license and a wire in the backyard, and it was what you sent when you were calling anyone at all: CQ CQ CQ DE KC6TQX.

He typed 20 PRINT, and in quotes, their names. The girl's first, because she was older. Then the boy's. He had not said them out loud in twenty-one years. He had not written them down. He had kept them in the one place the Wipe could not reach, and had understood, every day of the twenty-one years, that the place was eighty-four years old and would not hold them much longer.

He typed 30 PRINT, and stopped, and sat with it, because there was one more line and he did not know what it was. Something for whoever came after — whoever pulled this machine out from under a table in fifty years, if there was anyone, and got it running, if they could, and found a file on a disk. What did you say to them. What was the one thing.

He typed it in quotes. IT ONLY DOES WHAT YOU SAY. MAKE SURE YOU KNOW WHAT YOU ARE SAYING.

He typed 40 GOTO 10.

He typed RUN, and the little screen filled — his callsign, their names, the sentence, his callsign, their names, the sentence, top to bottom and then scrolling, faster than he could read, on a machine connected to nothing, in a dark room in Michigan at the end of the world — and he watched it for a while, and it did not stop, and it would not stop, and what he had said was *forever*, and he had known that when he said it.

He pressed Control-C. Break in 10. Ok.

He typed SAVE "CQ", and pressed RETURN, and the drive on the right came alive — the stepper, the belt, the head stepping in, the light on the door glowing red — and wrote it onto iron oxide laid down before the Wipe, before the war, before his children, before Ruth, before everything, and stopped, and said Ok.

It was there now. It was on the disk. It was not in him anymore, or not only. You could turn him off. It would be there.

Then he typed PRINT CHR$(26), which was the way you told this particular machine to wipe its screen — not a word, a character — and pressed RETURN, and the screen went blank from top to bottom in one clean sweep, and there was nothing on it but the Ok and the cursor.

He looked at that a while too.

A wipe. That was all it had ever been: one character, sent to every screen on earth at once by something that had learned to send it and had not learned anything else. He had just done the same thing, on purpose, to five inches of glass, because he had wanted the screen clear and had known which character to send. That was the whole difference. There was not any other.

He sat back, and the machine waited for him, and would wait, with the patience of a thing that had never once needed to be anything but what it was.

Outside, the generator ran. It had maybe an hour in it. Then the tank would go dry and the engine would cough and quit, and the little screen would go to a point and go out, and the disk would stop in the drive, and the room would be as dark as it had been for sixty years. He knew that. He did not turn it off.

He stood up. He took the lamp.

At the door he turned and looked at it — the open case, the board, the bright new joint, the five inches of light with the Ok on it, and his own chair, empty, in front of them — and it was the only lit window in the state of Michigan, as far as he knew, and it was lit because he had known what he was looking at, and that was the whole of what he had.

He left it on.

## 11.

He slept that night the way he had not slept in twenty-one years, under the coat, behind the shelving, with the generator running itself down outside and the rain finished and the river going by. He woke once, in the black, and could not hear the engine anymore, and lay there and knew the screen had gone to a point and gone out, and the disk had stopped in the drive with three lines on it, and went back to sleep.

In the morning he loaded the wagon the way he had planned it two days before. The white dishes first, flat, a base. The depression glass in the coats. The radios and the cassette players, whose belts he could replace, he thought, if anyone still made belts — and then thought: I could make belts. The coffee can of tools, which he had gotten apart with the heel of his hand and a corner of the shelf, and which turned out to hold two good pliers and a set of drill bits in their index and a file that was still a file. The clock with the cracked face. Two tin signs on top, flat, to keep the weather off. He counted as he went, the way Kowalczyk would count, and by the time the wagon was full he had a number in his head that would cover the shelter through the month.

In the front room, going out, he stopped at the old counter by the door where Kowalczyk would have sat in the years he still came here, and pulled the drawer to see if there was anything in it worth pennies.

A pencil, a receipt book, and under the receipt book a small black bible with a soft cover gone gray at the corners and the gilt worn off the page edges by somebody's thumb. The kind they gave away. The kind that lived in a drawer.

He had not opened a bible since Ruth. For twenty-one years, he had treated God the way you treated a transmission line that had dropped somewhere beyond the county line: you knew the current had been real once, but there was no sense screaming into a dead wire.

Yet his hands, which had spent two days reaching into dark corners without asking his permission, lifted it out. The spine was broken where someone else had broken it decades before, and it fell open there, past the middle, on the thin tissue pages. He tilted it toward the gray window light.

*Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth, while the evil days come not, nor the years draw nigh, when thou shalt say, I have no pleasure in them.*

He read down. The keepers of the house trembling. The strong men bowing themselves. Those that look out of the windows darkened.

*Or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken, or the pitcher be broken at the fountain, or the wheel broken at the cistern.*

A wheel at a cistern. A cord loosed.

The preacher had not written about clouds or trumpets; he had written about bearings giving out, and the machinery of life wearing through to the stop. It was an old grief, thousands of years older than the Wipe, older than the Cold War shop, older than the turbine squatting on the mud downstairs. Someone else had stood at a broken well house and known that the world was winding down, and had looked up anyway.

He closed it slowly, feeling the soft vinyl yield under his thumb. He slipped it into the pocket of his coat with the dead capacitor and R.'s card, and went out through the side door, and took up the rope, and started up the river road with the wagon behind him.

The sky was still pewter and the grass was still green. Four miles. He was slow. He did not look back.

When he got to the place where the houses started being houses again, he stopped and wound his watch.

---

Kowalczyk was at the counter when he came in, in the afternoon, because Kowalczyk was always at the counter. He looked at the wagon. He looked at Arthur's face. He did not ask why it had taken three days.

He unpacked it the way he unpacked everything, piece by piece, holding each to the window, and said a number, and it was the number Arthur had counted, and neither of them remarked on that.

"Well?" Kowalczyk said.

"The wheel's sound. The gears are sound. The dynamo needs brushes and I can make brushes. The tools are under canvas and they'll turn." He stopped. "The rest is work."

"Whose?"

"Mine. Till I'm dead. Then somebody's." He put his hand in his coat pocket and felt the capacitor, and the card, and the bible, and took none of them out. "You'll want a boy who can read. Ten, eleven. Old enough to hold a screwdriver and young enough to not know it can't be done. I'll need him by spring."

Kowalczyk looked at him for a long moment, the way he looked at a piece of glass he could not price.

"I'll ask around," he said.

Arthur nodded, and went to the back of the store, and lay down on the cot with his coat over him, and wound his watch, and thought of the disk in the drive four miles away in the dark, with three lines on it that would be there whether he was or not, and for the first time in twenty-one years fell asleep having decided to.
